# 🧩 Puzzles, Statement-Conclusion & Inequalities

---

# PART 1: PUZZLES (Floor / Order / Ranking)

## 🔴 TYPES

| Type | What it is |
|------|-----------|
| Floor puzzle | N people in N floors, assign who lives where |
| Ranking/order | Taller/heavier/faster than comparisons |
| Scheduling | Days of week, time slots, assignments |
| Multi-variable | Match people ↔ colours ↔ professions etc. |

---

## 🔴 SOLVING APPROACH

1. Make a **grid/table** immediately
2. Put definite clues first (exact positions)
3. Use elimination for remaining
4. Use "not" clues to cross off cells
5. Never assume — only what's stated

---

## 🟠 SOLVED EXAMPLES — FLOOR PUZZLE

### Example 1
> A, B, C, D, E live on floors 1–5 (1=ground, 5=top). A is above C. B is on the top floor. D is immediately below A. E is on the ground floor. Find everyone's floor.

B = 5 (top)
E = 1 (ground)
Remaining: A, C, D for floors 2, 3, 4
D is immediately below A → D=A−1
A is above C

Try A=4, D=3: C must be below A → C=2 ✓ (only remaining)

**Result: E=1, C=2, D=3, A=4, B=5**

---

### Example 2 — Ranking
> A is taller than B. C is taller than A. D is shorter than B. E is shorter than D.

Chain: C > A > B > D > E
Tallest = C, Shortest = **E**

---

## 🟡 PRACTICE — PUZZLES

**P1.** 6 people A–F in floors 1–6. A is on an even floor. B is directly above C. D is on floor 4. E is above D. F is below C. B is not on the top floor. Arrange all.

**P2.** P is heavier than Q. R is lighter than S. T is heavier than S. R is heavier than P. Heaviest to lightest order?

**P3.** 5 people sit for exam on Mon–Fri. A sits on Wednesday. B sits before A but not on Monday. C sits on a day immediately after B. D sits on Friday. E sits on the remaining day.

# PART 2: STATEMENT & CONCLUSION / ASSUMPTIONS

## 🔴 TYPES OF QUESTIONS

| Type | What to check |
|------|--------------|
| **Valid Conclusion** | Does the statement definitely imply this? |
| **Valid Assumption** | Is this taken for granted in the statement? |
| **Course of Action** | Is this a practical response to the problem? |
| **Cause & Effect** | Does A cause B or B cause A? |

---

## 🔴 RULES

### Valid Conclusion Rules
- Must follow **directly** from the statement
- Cannot contradict the statement
- Cannot be too broad (extreme words = risky: all, never, always, none)
- Cannot bring in outside information

### Valid Assumption Rules
- Something **implied but not stated**
- Must be necessary for the statement to make sense
- Avoid assumptions that are too obvious or too extreme

### Course of Action Rules
- Must be **practical** and **directly address** the problem
- Both/neither/one can be valid
- Extreme or irrelevant actions are invalid

---

## 🟠 SOLVED EXAMPLES

### Example 1 — Conclusion
> Statement: "All students who study hard pass the exam."
> I: Students who don't study hard fail. II: Some students who pass studied hard.

I = Inverse of statement → NOT necessarily valid (could pass other ways) ❌
II = Directly implied by the statement ✅
**Only II follows.**

### Example 2 — Assumption
> Statement: "Join our English speaking course to get a good job."
> Assumption I: Good knowledge of English helps in getting a job.
> Assumption II: People want good jobs.

I = Necessary assumption for the ad to make sense ✅
II = Too general, but implied ✅
**Both assumptions follow.**

### Example 3 — Course of action
> "The number of accidents at a railway crossing has increased."
> I: The railway crossing should be closed permanently.
> II: A traffic police officer should be posted at the crossing.

I = Too extreme — closing permanently not practical ❌
II = Direct practical solution ✅
**Only II follows.**

---

## 🟡 PRACTICE — STATEMENT-CONCLUSION

**P1.** Statement: "Most people who exercise regularly live longer."
I: Exercise is the only factor for long life.
II: Regular exercise contributes to longevity.

**P2.** Statement: "The bank offers 8% interest on savings accounts."
Assumption I: People prefer higher interest rates.
Assumption II: The bank wants to attract more deposits.

**P3.** Statement: "Crime rate in the city has increased by 30% this year."
Course I: Police should increase night patrols.
Course II: The city should be evacuated immediately.

# PART 3: INEQUALITIES

## 🔴 SYMBOLS

| Symbol | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| > | Greater than |
| < | Less than |
| ≥ | Greater than or equal |
| ≤ | Less than or equal |
| = | Equal |

---

## 🔴 CHAIN RULE

Given: A > B ≥ C = D < E

You can conclude:
- A > C ✅ (A>B and B≥C → A>C)
- A > D ✅
- A vs E → cannot determine ❌ (D<E but A's relation to E unknown)
- C = D ✅ (directly stated)

**Rule:** If chain is unbroken, you can conclude. If there's a break (e.g., both ≥ and ≤ in the same chain), may not be able to conclude.

---

## 🔴 EITHER/OR IN INEQUALITIES

If neither A>B nor A<B follows individually, check:
- If A>B and A<B are complementary (opposite) → Either A>B or A<B follows

But if A=B is possible → neither "A>B" nor "A<B" alone is guaranteed.

---

## 🟠 SOLVED EXAMPLES

### Example 1
> A > B, B ≥ C, C = D, D < E
> I: A > D. II: E > B. III: A > E.

I: A>B≥C=D → A>D ✅
II: E>D=C≤B, and E>D but B≥C, no clear B vs E relation ❌
III: A vs E — no chain connects them directly ❌
**Only I follows.**

### Example 2
> P ≥ Q > R, S < Q, T = P
> I: T > R. II: S < P.

T=P≥Q>R → T>R ✅
S<Q≤P → S<P ✅
**Both follow.**

---

## 🟡 PRACTICE — INEQUALITIES

**P1.** A≥B>C=D>E. Which is definitely true?
I: A>E. II: B>D. III: A=D.

**P2.** P<Q, Q≤R, R>S, S≥T.
I: P<R. II: T<Q. III: R>T.

**P3.** M>N=O≥P, Q>M.
I: Q>P. II: N>P. III: Q>O.

---

## ❌ COMMON MISTAKES

- Assuming you can conclude across a "gap" in inequality chain
- Accepting extreme conclusions in Statement-Conclusion questions
- In courses of action: accepting extreme/impractical actions
- Confusing ≥ (can be equal) with > (strictly greater)
