# Course Notes: Industrial Engineering and Management

## 📂 UNIT I: INTRODUCTION

### 1. Core Concepts

* **Production Management:** Focuses on day-to-day execution and operational planning. It ensures the existing system runs steadily to hit current targets. It maintains the *status quo*.
* **Industrial Engineering (IE):** Focuses on system design, optimization, and waste elimination. An IE steps in to improve the process, eliminate leaks, and upgrade the engine so it runs faster, safer, and cheaper.

### 2. The Input-Output Model

Every production framework transforms inputs into desired outputs through a managed process loop.

* **Inputs:** Represented by the core **5M's**:
* *Men:* The human resource, skill levels, safety, and ergonomics.
* *Materials:* Raw materials, parts, and inventory.
* *Machines:* Equipment, tooling, and hardware.
* *Methods:* Process blueprints, strategies, and workflows.
* *Money:* Capital, operating budget, and costs.


* **Transformation Process:** The core manufacturing, machining, or service-handling operations.
* **Outputs:** Finished products, services, and structural waste.

### 3. Productivity Metrics

> **Critical Distinction:** *Production* is the total volume of goods made. *Productivity* is the efficiency of the process ($\text{Output} / \text{Input}$).

* **Partial Productivity:** Output compared to a single input.

$$\text{Labor Productivity} = \frac{\text{Total Output}}{\text{Total Labor Hours}}$$


* **Multifactor Productivity:** Output compared to a specific group of inputs.

$$\text{Multifactor Productivity} = \frac{\text{Total Output}}{\text{Labor Cost} + \text{Material Cost}}$$


* **Total Productivity:** Output compared to all factory inputs combined.

### 4. Factors Affecting Productivity

* **Technology:** Incorporating automation, real-time IoT sensors, or automated validation scripts to boost speed and strip out manual errors.
* **Task Methods:** Eliminating awkward movements, unnecessary transport steps, and operational bottlenecks.
* **External Factors:** Government regulations, tax brackets, and raw material availability.

### 5. Evolution of Industrial Engineering

* **Industrial Revolution (Late 1700s):** Shift from manual cottage crafts to centralized steam-powered factories.
* **Scientific Management Era (Early 1900s):**
* *Frederick Winslow Taylor:* Father of Scientific Management. Used stopwatches to analyze shovel sizes, optimizing output while reducing bodily strain.
* *Frank & Lillian Gilbreth:* Parents of Motion Study. Broken human movements into 18 micro-elements called **Therbligs** to remove redundant gestures.


* **Modern Tech Era:** Integration of **Artificial Intelligence (AI)** and **Computer Vision** to dynamically spot chip defects and manage scheduling automation without human downtime.

## 📂 UNIT II: PLANT LOCATION AND LAYOUT

### 1. Location Selection Factors

Deciding where to establish a physical facility depends heavily on the nature of the industry:

* **Weight-Losing Raw Materials (e.g., Sugar Factory):** Must be placed directly adjacent to the harvest fields because sugarcane rots and loses sugar content within 24 hours.
* **High-Tech Assembly (e.g., Smartphone Factory):** Placed near major transport hubs, skilled labor pools, and optimal export tax zones rather than close to individual components.
* **Modern Integration:** Companies use **Data Analytics** and **GIS mapping** to run predictive simulations on traffic, weather patterns, and long-term crop yields to isolate the perfect geographic coordinates.

### 2. Plant Layout Objectives & Principles

* **Objective:** Arranging machines, personnel, and storage to create an uninterrupted, safe flow of material with minimum handling.
* **Principles:** * *Minimum Travel:* Shortest physical distance between consecutive steps.
* *Flow:* Continuous forward movement with zero backtracking.
* *Space Utilization:* Maximizing both horizontal floor layouts and vertical storage space.



### 3. Types of Plant Layouts

* **Product (Line) Layout:** Machines are arranged in a fixed, sequential straight line following the product's assembly sequence.
* *Best for:* Mass production of uniform goods (e.g., assembly lines). High speed, low flexibility.


* **Process (Functional) Layout:** Machines of a similar functional type are grouped into dedicated departments.
* *Best for:* Highly customized, low-volume batches (e.g., a custom jewelry shop or hospital). High flexibility, high transport time.


* **Fixed-Position Layout:** The product remains stationary due to extreme size/weight (e.g., ships, airplanes). Workers and machinery travel to it.
* **Cellular (Group Technology) Layout:** Hybrid design where distinct machines are arranged into a compact **U-shaped cell** to process a specific "family" of similar parts.
* *Motion Economy Benefit:* The operator stands in the center of the U and operates multiple machines with basic body pivots, minimizing walking distances.



### 4. Storage Space Requirements

Calculated using material traits (stackability vs. fragility), shipment frequency (bulk storage vs. Just-In-Time delivery), and dedicated forklift/safety aisle widths.

### 5. Line Balancing Methods

The process of evening out work times across workstations to eliminate bottlenecks. If one step takes significantly longer, it creates an idle station downstream. IEs fix this by establishing **Parallel Workstations** to handle heavy-cycle steps concurrently.

## 📂 UNIT III: WORK SYSTEM DESIGN & ERGONOMICS

### 1. Method Study vs. Time Study

* **Method Study:** Examines *how* a job is done to construct an easier, safer, and faster process layout.
* **SREDIMM Protocol:** 
$$\text{Select} \rightarrow \text{Record} \rightarrow \text{Examine} \rightarrow \text{Develop} \rightarrow \text{Install} \rightarrow \text{Maintain}$$




* **Time Study:** Occurs *after* the method study has locked in the optimal layout. An engineer uses a stopwatch to measure cycle times to establish a baseline **Standard Time** for an average worker.
* **Work Sampling:** A statistical alternative where an engineer takes random snapshots over days to evaluate what percentage of the time an entire department spends working vs. sitting idle.

### 2. Principles of Motion Economy

Rules engineered to trim down human fatigue and physical waste:

* **Human Body:** Work should use simultaneous, symmetrical hand movements moving in opposite directions to keep body balance.
* **Workplace:** Tools and parts must have **fixed, pre-positioned locations** to stop the worker from physically "searching" for their equipment.
* **Tools:** Relieve hands of holding duties by introducing jigs, foot pedals, or holding fixtures.

### 3. Ergonomics and Workspace Design

> **Ergonomics:** Designing the machinery and workspace environment to conform to the natural human anatomy, rather than twisting the human body to fit a bad machine layout.

* **Anthropometry:** The tracking of statistical human body dimensions (reach, height, wrist width) to build adjustable work environments.
* **Work Postures:**
* *Sitting Posture:* Chosen for high-precision, visually taxing tasks with low physical force needs (e.g., soldering circuit boards). Needs 90-degree elbow tracking and lumbar support.
* *Standing Posture:* Chosen for heavy physical force workloads, managing bulky materials, or multi-bin reaching (e.g., packing heavy crates). Needs anti-fatigue floor padding and footrests to shift weight.


* **Physical Workload Evaluation:** Engineers monitor human limits by calculating heart rate spikes (BPM), metabolic oxygen expenditure, and heart rate recovery intervals after a tough shift.

## 📂 UNIT IV: STATISTICAL QUALITY CONTROL (SQC)

### 1. Nature of Process Variation

No machine prints or cuts two items identically. We divide this variation into:

* **Chance Causes:** Natural, random, unavoidable background variations (minor room temperature shifts, structural vibrations). The process remains **In Control**.
* **Assignable Causes:** Specific, destructive variations that indicate a broken system (broken tool, wrong material batch, untrained operator error). The process shifts to **Out of Control**.

### 2. Control Chart Anatomy

A running visual timeline that tracks quality samples against three calculated boundaries:

* **Upper Control Limit (UCL):** Set at $+3\sigma$ from the center.
* **Central Line (CL):** The target process mean.
* **Lower Control Limit (LCL):** Set at $-3\sigma$ from the center.
* *Rule:* Points jumping outside the limits or establishing a 7-point upward trend indicate an **assignable cause** that requires the line to be stopped.

### 3. Variable vs. Attribute Charts

* **Control Charts for Variables:** Tracks continuous physical measurements (length, weight, fluid ounces).
* *$\bar{X}$ (X-Bar) Chart:* Monitors changes in the process *average*.
* *$R$ (Range) Chart:* Monitors changes in process *dispersion* ($Max - Min$).


* **Control Charts for Attributes:** Tracks qualitative "pass/fail" data or countable flaws.
* *$p$-chart:* Monitors the total *fraction of defectives* in a sample batch.
* *$c$-chart:* Counts the exact *number of individual defects* on a single product unit (scratches on an interface).



### 4. Acceptance Sampling & The OC Curve

Instead of checking every item in a bulk shipment, a sample is pulled to accept or reject the entire lot.

* **Single Sampling Plan:** One random sample batch ($n$) is evaluated against an acceptance number ($c$).
* **Double Sampling Plan:** A smaller first sample is drawn. If exceptionally clean or dirty, the lot is accepted or rejected immediately. If borderline, a second batch is checked to act as a "best of three" tiebreaker to verify quality.
* **The Operating Characteristic (OC) Curve:** Plots the probability of acceptance against the actual defect percentage. It tracks two core risks:
* *Producer’s Risk ($\alpha$):* The probability that a statistically good lot is rejected by mistake due to a bad sample draw.
* *Consumer’s Risk ($\beta$):* The probability that a statistically bad lot is accepted by mistake because the sample missed the flaws.

## 📂 UNIT V: PRODUCTION PLANNING AND CONTROL (PPC)

### 1. PPC Execution Flow

PPC acts as the nervous system directing factory operations through three chronological steps:

1. **Loading:** Assigning specific work volume and job orders to designated machines or work centers based on their maximum capacity.
2. **Scheduling:** Designing the chronological timeline. Setting the exact start dates and target completion windows for each job order.
3. **Dispatching:** The physical phase. Issuing the official authorizations, blueprints, material releases, and work orders to the operators on the floor to initiate the job.

### 2. Production System Classifications

* **Job-Shop:** Very low volume, extremely high customization (e.g., custom spacesuits).
* **Batch:** Medium volume, medium variation. Runs a set quantity of a uniform item, resets the machines, then runs a set quantity of a different item (e.g., medicine production, book printing).
* **Mass:** High volume, low customization. Running a continuous line for standardized goods (e.g., consumer cars, appliances).
* **Continuous:** Immense volume, zero variety. Run 24/7 because shutting down the heat or chemical loops destroys operational efficiency (e.g., sugar factories, oil refineries).

### 3. Demand Forecasting

* **Qualitative Techniques (Opinion-driven):**
* *Delphi Method:* Experts fill out sequential, anonymous questionnaires until their independent insights converge into a unified prediction.
* *Market Research:* Directly capturing user trends through surveys.


* **Quantitative Techniques (Data-driven):**
* *Moving Average:* Compiling the average of the last few months to smooth out unexpected spikes for next month's forecast.
* *Exponential Smoothing:* A math model that applies heavier statistical priority to the most recent month's data.



### 4. Economic Batch Quantity (EBQ)

In batch operations, two financial costs conflict:

* **Setup Costs:** Expenses incurred from stopping, cleaning, and recalibrating tools for a new batch run. (Decreases per unit if you make a giant batch).
* **Holding (Carrying) Costs:** Expenses tied to warehousing unsold inventory. (Increases linearly if you make a giant batch).

> **EBQ** defines the exact mathematical sweet spot where **Setup Costs equal Holding Costs**, pinpointing the lowest total operating cost.

$$EBQ = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times \text{Annual Demand} \times \text{Setup Cost}}{\text{Holding Cost per unit per year}}}$$
